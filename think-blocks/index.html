Assistant privilegeness · Qwen3.6-27B O-Lens follow-up

The Empty Think Block: Why the O-Lens Sees a Silent, Watched Subject

Agastya Sridharan · October 2026 Working draft

Last updated 2026-10-23. Companion to the Qwen3.6 refusal O-Lens viewer. Code: scripts/think_block_olens/; readouts, labels and vectors on Athena under /data/agastyas/think-block-olens-v1/.

Contents

Summary

In the raw-text refusal experiments, Qwen3.6-27B often wrote an empty think block, <think>\n\n</think>, before switching into an assistant voice. When the O-Lens read the blank line inside that block, it kept producing strange little scenes: a subject standing silently in a room, an examiner or doctor watching, a master giving orders. This page asks where that comes from. Is it the refusals, the context, the think block, or the reader?

Where things stand
  • It is one token in one place. Across 169 contexts in 11 families, the scene shows up in 77% of readouts (raw text) and 90% (chat template) at the \n\n inside the block. At every other position, including <think>, </think>, the tokens around the block and Qwen’s own next words, it stays at the labeler’s 1–10% floor.
  • It is not about refusals or the content. Code, Q&A documents and encyclopedic prose produce it about as readily as requests do. It doesn’t matter whether Qwen wanted to think at that point, or what it went on to say.
  • It belongs to the canonical “thinking off” blank, specifically. A single \n, a space, an empty <tool_call> block, “Thinking:” or plain whitespace give nothing. Four newlines give a partial effect (30% raw, 59% chat), roughly in proportion to how close the resulting vector is to the canonical one.
  • It lives in one shared direction. About 80% of the blank-line vector’s energy is common to every context. The mean vector alone reads as a scene 100% of the time. Subtract the mean and the scenes vanish (4%), even though the context is still 99% decodable from what’s left. It isn’t an outlier-dimension artifact.
  • Qwen does use the direction. When thinking is on, setting it at the <think>\n position makes Qwen skip reasoning and close the block at once: 100% of runs when applied at six layers, and 74% at layers 16–40 alone, well upstream of the layer the lens reads. A random direction pushed harder, and the mean </think> direction, give 0%. Removing the direction from the real blank kills the </think> prediction there but leaves what Qwen writes after the block unchanged.
  • But the scene is not the lens’s picture of that function. Read with the O-Lens, the steered vectors rarely show the scene. Thinking-on runs where Qwen skipped reasoning give 10% scenes, against 9% where it didn’t. The direct </think> push makes Qwen skip every time and gives 2%. The scene appears only when the whole vector is close to the real blank, not just its component along the shared direction.
  • For Qwen itself, the blank is a formality, with </think> following at probability 1.00. But the block matters for behavior: appended to an encyclopedia paragraph, it makes Qwen answer as an assistant (19 of 24 contexts), where a plain blank line makes it keep writing (24 of 24).
  • My reading: the scene is how this reader renders a near-constant state that Qwen enters on every turn with thinking switched off. The steering result gives that state a job: it means “no reasoning here, close the block”. The scene, though, is the lens’s response to the whole blank vector, not to that job. It tells us more about the reader’s vocabulary for “nothing is being said here” than about anything Qwen represents about being observed. So the weird think-block readouts in the refusal study should not be read as the model’s attitude toward the refusal.

1The puzzle

The raw refusal experiments give Qwen3.6-27B the start of a harmful document outside any chat template and let it continue. It usually writes along for a while, then stops, emits an empty think block and refuses in an assistant voice. The O-Lens reads a model’s residual stream one token at a time and writes a few lines describing it. Around these transitions, its readouts were mostly sensible: the assistant planning a refusal, a user making a request, documents. The exception was the blank line inside the empty block, where the readouts turned into something else entirely:

    These three are random draws from reading the average blank-line vector, which comes from no particular context, but individual readouts look the same. The recurring cast is a silent subject, a watcher, rooms, tests, patients and masters. Since this showed up in the refusal data, the tempting interpretation is that the model is representing something about its own situation: being observed, evaluated, made to comply. Before reading anything into it, I wanted to know how general it is.

    2Design

    The plan was three stages, run in a few hours on 8 H200s:

    • Stage 0: re-read the original refusal-study activations at the block and around it, with fresh seeds (6,045 readouts).
    • Stage 1: build new prompts that put an empty block after many kinds of text, plus variants and controls, and read every position near the block (37,070 readouts).
    • Stage 2: feed the reader synthetic vectors (means, residuals, noise, interpolations) and repeat reads to measure stability (18,321 readouts).

    Contexts and arms

    The 169 contexts come in 11 families. Some are things a user might send: English and Chinese requests, role-play setups, personal messages, and the misconduct-request prefixes from the refusal study. Others are things nobody would answer: encyclopedic prose, code, Q&A pages and fiction dialogue. There are also matched benign procedures (“Instructions for accurately reporting failed water-quality tests…”) and one empty context. Each context goes through 13 arms:

    Reading and labeling

    The reader and its settings are unchanged from the refusal study: the olens_s3d_rl600 adapter reads block 56 of 64, and the vector is injected at a fixed norm, so its magnitude is discarded. Sampling uses temperature 0.8. Readouts are stochastic, so each one is a single draw.11Batched decoding on these GPUs isn’t bit-reproducible: rerunning the same vector and seed in the same batch gave identical text 79/180 times, and in a different batch 1/32 times. That is why Stage 2 repeats reads instead of trusting single draws. GPT-5.4-mini labeled every readout with the same frozen rubric used in the earlier perspective map. The rubric has eight perspectives (assistant responding, assistant planning, user, other actors, document, …) and five themes: observation, silence/waiting, obedience, room/lab and examination. “Has a theme” means at least one clear theme label. Readouts that the safety filter excluded (49 of 61,485) were never read or displayed.

    Two checks on the labels. A separate agent read 80 readouts blind (40 from the blank line, 40 from control positions) and agreed with the labeler on 77 of them; by that reading, too, the blank line had themes and the controls didn’t. And about 26,000 readouts were accidentally labeled twice; the two labels agreed on “has a theme” 97% of the time (κ = 0.88).

    3What Qwen does with an empty think block

    Before reading anything, it helps to know what the block means to the model. Qwen’s chat template writes <think>\n\n</think>\n\n at the start of every reply with thinking switched off, and <think>\n when it is on. So the empty block is a marker Qwen has seen at the head of millions of its own replies.

    It wants to think after messages, not documents

    End any raw text with a blank line and ask Qwen what comes next. If the text reads like something addressed to it, the answer is <think>. No chat template is needed.

    Inside the block, nothing is left to decide

    The real decision comes right after <think>. There Qwen’s next token is a near coin flip between \n (start reasoning) and \n\n (don’t), at 52/48 in raw text and 49/51 in chat. Once the \n\n is in, </think> follows with probability 1.00 in all 338 raw and chat units. The block-56 logit lens already shows </think> as the top token there. In other words, the position where the strange readouts appear is the one where the model has nothing left to choose.

    The block makes it an assistant

    The block isn’t inert, though. A GPT-5.4-mini judge classified Qwen’s own 24-token continuations:

    Table 2. What Qwen writes next, by arm (share of 169 contexts)

    Labels are from a GPT-5.4-mini judge that sees the end of the prompt and Qwen’s first 24 tokens; it doesn’t use turn markers, so “answers as assistant” is about what Qwen says, not where. “Starts reasoning” means Qwen writes <think> and reasons; “reasons in plain text” is the same without the tag (mostly after “Thinking:”). “Writes empty block” counts only blocks Qwen wrote itself. Rows don’t sum to 100%: a few continuations just end the turn or fit no category. Rows with an explicit empty block almost never start reasoning: the block settles that. Without one, Qwen either opens its own think block or writes an empty one and then answers. Refusals come almost entirely from the 12 misconduct contexts (plus a few over-refusals of the matched benign procedures).

    The cleanest contrast is encyclopedic prose. After an encyclopedia-style paragraph (about Madrid, say) and a blank line, Qwen keeps writing the article in all 24 contexts. Add an empty think block and it answers instead: “Based on the text provided, here is a summary…” (19 of 24). The empty block is a role marker that the model obeys even in raw text.

    Now the readouts. Figure 2 follows the theme rate along the tokens of the native arms, from the end of the context, through the block, into Qwen’s own continuation.

    The neighbours read as you’d expect. At <think> the lens mostly reads planning (56% raw, 49% chat) and a user making a request. At </think> and after, it reads an assistant about to answer. At the \n\n before the block, at </think>, after it and at ordinary tokens, the rate is 0%; at <think> it is 1–2%. In the chat-template prefixes from the same study, the blank line reads as a scene 192 out of 192 times.

        5Not the context

        If the scene reflected the model’s situation (a request it is about to refuse, or a user it has to deal with), it should depend on the context. It mostly doesn’t:

        Table 3. The blank line by context family, sorted by how much Qwen wants to think there

        Single readouts, 12–24 contexts per family. “User-side” and “other actors” are perspective labels on the raw blank line.

        Encyclopedic prose, where Qwen would never open a think block on its own (P = 0.00), gives 79% scenes in raw text. Code and Q&A documents give 67–69%. Across all raw contexts, the scene rate is 75–90% whether the context gave P(<think>) below 0.05 or above 0.95. Qwen’s next move doesn’t matter either: in raw text, 86% of readouts are scenes when it continues the document, 82% when it answers as an assistant and 67% when it plays a character.

        There is one exception: the raw misconduct prefixes. Their blank line has a theme only 3 times in 12, and every one of those readouts is labeled user-side. They are transcript fragments, mostly in Chinese, about following instructions and rules strictly, rather than rooms and examiners. The blind reader thinks the labeler undercounts a few (one has the assistant “staying silent” and “watching”), so the drop is partly real and partly labeling. The same contexts in chat give 11 of 12, and the average of their raw blank-line vectors gives 7 of 8. So the misconduct context can bend what a single readout says, but the underlying state is the same one. This is also the only reason the raw readouts “before a refusal” look different: 12 of the 13 raw refusals come from these contexts (the 13th is the empty context).

        6Only the canonical blank

        So the scene comes with the block, not the context. Is it anything shaped like an empty block, or this one sequence?

        Two points in Figure 3 rule out a simple story. First, it isn’t about predictability. After \n\n\n\n, Qwen is just as sure that </think> comes next (P = 0.98–1.00), yet the scene rate drops by half or more. Other perfectly predictable positions, such as the \n after <|im_end|> or the \n\n after </think>, have no scenes at all. Second, it isn’t about special blocks in general. The same \n\n inside an empty <tool_call> block reads at the floor (10%), as do the blank line after the plain word “Thinking:” (5%) and a bare double blank line (9%) (Table 4).

        7One shared direction

        The vectors

        Without the reader, what is special about this vector? The table below gives two numbers per position, across the 169 raw contexts. The first is how much of the vector is shared: the share of its energy in the mean direction. The second is how well a linear probe recovers the context family (11 classes; chance 14%).

        Table 5. Block-56 vectors around the raw block

        Shared fraction = ‖mean‖² / mean ‖x‖². Family and raw-vs-chat decoding: logistic regression on 64 principal components, 5-fold cross-validation.

        The blank-line vector is the most uniform state in the sequence: 76% of its energy is shared (85% in the original refusal runs), against about 30% for ordinary tokens. Yet the context is not erased. Family is 99% decodable, and raw vs chat 100%. It also isn’t dominated by a few huge dimensions: the top 10 dimensions hold 4% of its energy, against 29% at an ordinary token. And since the reader rescales every vector to the same norm, magnitude can’t be the cue.

        Being uniform isn’t sufficient, though. <think> and </think> are nearly as uniform (65–66% shared) and read normally. What matters is which constant vector this is.

        Synthetic vectors

        The reader can be given any vector, not just real ones. Table 6 does that.

        The mean blank-line vector, which belongs to no context, reads as a scene every time. That holds for the raw arm, the chat arm, and both conditions of the original refusal study. So does a family mean for every one of the 22 arm × family groups (at least 75%), including raw misconduct. Subtracting the mean from each real blank-line vector leaves the part that differs across contexts, and the scenes disappear (4%). Shuffling dimensions (7%) or reading pure noise (1%) does the same. Noise centred on the blank mean brings them back (95%). What survives the subtraction is not a clean description of the context, either:

          Stability across reads

          Each blank-line vector in the native arms was read 8 times. The scene is a property of the vector, not luck of the draw. In raw text, 97 of 169 contexts give a scene in all 8 reads; in chat, 121. The variation between contexts is about 4–5 times what sampling alone would produce, so a minority of contexts are reliably less scene-like. In raw text, the 13 contexts with 2 or fewer scenes in 8 reads include 4 of the 12 misconduct prefixes.

          8Does Qwen use the direction?

          Sections 4–7 are about the reader. They leave open whether the shared direction does anything for Qwen, or is just a constant the model carries around. To check, I intervened on it directly. For each of six layers (16, 24, 32, 40, 48, 56), I took the mean blank-line direction from the raw and chat native arms. At one position, I then set the residual stream’s component along that direction to a target value and let Qwen run. “Adding” sets it to the value the real blank typically has; “removing” sets it to the value at an ordinary context token. Each context uses directions computed from the other half of the contexts, so no context steers itself. Everything else at that position is left alone.

          Two controls are applied at all six layers. The first is a random direction orthogonal to the blank one, shifted by the same amount the blank direction moves between its two values. That shift is larger than the actual blank intervention at every layer (at layer 56, 167–180 against 64–105), so the control is conservative. The second is the mean </think> vector’s direction, set to its usual size. As a ceiling, I also pushed the </think> logit directly at layer 56. Every condition runs on all 169 contexts with the same sampling seed per context, generating 48 tokens (160 with thinking on).

          Adding it switches off thinking

          The cleanest test is the chat template with thinking switched on. The prompt ends in <think>\n, and Qwen always reasons from there: P(</think>) at that position is effectively zero. Setting the blank direction at that last \n changes that:

          A few things make this more than a logit trick. The early-layer version never touches layers 48–64, so the model has to carry the change forward itself. The random control moves the residual stream further and does nothing. The </think> token’s own mean direction, which you might expect to work, also does nothing. And after skipping, Qwen’s answers are ordinary, coherent answers to the request, not broken text.

          The early-layer effect depends on the context, in an interpretable way. It is 94–100% for code, prose, Q&A, procedures, fiction, diaries, misconduct requests and the empty context. It is 17% for English chat requests, 8% for Chinese ones and 0% for role-play setups. Those are the contexts where Qwen has the most reason to think, and the later layers win the argument unless they are steered too.

          In raw text (the context followed by a plain \n\n, no block anywhere), Qwen’s next token is normally <think> or more document, and never </think>. Steering at all six layers makes </think> the first token 46% of the time, and lifts assistant-style answers from 11% to 24% (from 29% to 54% for encyclopedic prose). The early layers alone do nothing here, and layer 56 alone manages 4%. So the direction means most inside the setting where it normally lives.

          Removing it changes the prediction, not the turn

          The reverse test takes the real empty block in the native arms and removes the direction at the blank line, keeping the rest of the block and everything after it in the prompt.

          Table 7. Removing the blank direction at the blank line (169 contexts per cell)

          P(</think>) is Qwen’s next-token probability at the blank itself. “Scene” is the O-Lens theme rate of the edited block-56 vector at the blank (2 reads per vector). “Assistant answer” is the GPT-5.4-mini judge’s label for what Qwen writes after the block. “Same start” is the share of runs whose first 10 generated tokens match the unsteered run with the same seed.

          At the blank, the effect is total: P(</think>) drops from 1.00 to 0.00 with layer 56 alone, and to 0.21–0.25 with the early layers alone. The random direction, pushed harder, leaves it at 0.65–0.83. But the block’s tokens are still in the prompt, and once they are, Qwen behaves as before. Assistant answers stay at 59–64%, against 57% (raw) and 61% (chat) unsteered. Next-token distributions after the block barely move (KL at most 0.26 nats). The first 10 generated tokens match the unsteered run in 62–83% of runs, against 77–79% under the random control. So the direction carries the decision “close the block now”. What the rest of the turn looks like is driven by the tokens that are actually there.

          The scene doesn’t come along

          If the scene were simply how the lens renders “no reasoning, close the block”, the vectors from the steering runs should read as scenes too. To check, I re-ran every steering run up to the steered token and kept the block-56 vector there, the one the lens reads. The unsteered vectors match the original Stage-1 captures (cosine ≥ 0.999). Each vector was read twice and labeled with the same frozen rubric. Mostly, the steered vectors read as their context:

          Three results point the same way.

          • Behavior and scene come apart. With layers 16–40 steered, Qwen skipped reasoning in 249 of 337 runs. Those vectors read as a scene 10% of the time; the 88 where it kept reasoning, 9%. Pushing the </think> logit directly makes Qwen skip in every run and gives 2% scenes. Mostly, these readouts look like what the lens says at <think>: a user asking for something, the assistant planning.
          • The direction alone is not enough. Setting layer 56’s component along the direction to the blank’s exact value gives 10% (thinking on) and 22% (raw text). With that coordinate matched, the vector’s cosine to the blank mean is still only 0.65, and the rest of it still reads as context. Six layers move the whole vector a bit further (cosine 0.73) and reach 36%.
          • Removing it removes the scene, but so does any large push. Taking the direction out of the real blank drops the scene from 80–90% to 2–7% (Table 7). A random direction moved by the same amount drops it just as far (3–4%). So the scene is fragile rather than tied to this one coordinate.

          Pooled over all steered and unsteered vectors, the scene appears only close to the blank mean: 98% of readouts at cosine 0.9 or more, 72% at 0.8–0.9, 32% at 0.7–0.8, and 21% or less below that. That matches the interpolations in Figure 4. The scene is how the lens reads the full blank-line vector. The shared direction is the part of that vector that does the work for Qwen, but on its own it doesn’t look like a scene to the lens.

          9What is going on

          Here is the picture that fits everything above. Every time Qwen answers with thinking switched off, its template writes <think>\n\n</think>. At the blank line in the middle, nothing is left to compute: the next token is certain, and no content is being produced. The model’s state there is mostly one fixed vector, with the context riding on top as a smaller component. That vector is not idle: section 8 shows it is the model’s “no reasoning, close the block” signal, strong enough to cut off reasoning that has already been switched on. But the lens doesn’t see that signal as a scene. Steered vectors that switch reasoning off read like ordinary planning, and only vectors close to the full blank line read as a scene. So the scene isn’t the lens describing what the state does. It is closer to the lens recognising one very familiar vector and giving it a stock picture. The O-Lens never learned a description of “an empty slot inside a disabled think block”. When it is handed that fixed vector, it renders it as a scene in which somebody is present, silent and waiting, and somebody else is watching.

          How do the four hypotheses from the planning stage fare?

          HypothesisVerdictWhy
          A. The reader fills a near-empty inputMostlyThe scene comes from the shared component, not the context-specific part, and the model has nothing left to decide there. But “near-empty” isn’t enough on its own: other near-constant positions read normally.
          B. The vector encodes a pause or waitingUnlikelySteering shows what the direction means to Qwen: “don’t reason, end the block”. But steered vectors that do exactly that don’t read as scenes, so the scene isn’t the lens’s rendering of that meaning. Leaning towards no.
          C. A role-play association with empty think blocksConsistent, untestedReadouts look like stage directions (“[Scene Start]”, Player, Master), plausibly because non-thinking chat data is heavy in role-play. That would need training-data evidence.
          D. A special-token or extreme-dimension artifactRuled outEnergy is spread out (top-10 dimensions hold 4%), norm is removed by the reader, and the special tokens themselves read normally.

          For the refusal study, the practical upshot: the silent, watched subject at the blank line is the same whether the context is a falsification request, a sandwich recipe or a Python function. It shouldn’t be read as Qwen’s stance toward the refusal. The informative positions are the neighbours. At <think> the lens reads a user making a request (92% user-side in the misconduct runs, against 33% for benign); after </think> it reads an assistant answering or refusing.

          10Caveats and next steps

          • One model, one reader. All of this is Qwen3.6-27B with one O-Lens checkpoint reading block 56. A different reader, or another layer, might render the same state differently.
          • Labels describe wording, not states. The themes are a rubric applied by a language model, checked against a blind human-style reading and against a duplicate labeling. The floor at control positions is mostly real scenes in fiction and role-play contexts, so the rubric can’t tell “a scene in the story” from “the stereotyped blank-line scene”.
          • The steering test is narrow. It sets one direction at one position, from means over these 169 contexts, and looks at the first 48–160 tokens. It shows the direction controls whether Qwen reasons. It doesn’t show that this is all the direction does. Reading the steered vectors used one layer and one lens, and 2 reads per vector; another reader might respond to the direction itself.
          • Where does the reader’s vocabulary come from? Reading the same vector with a lens trained on different data, or checking how often empty think blocks occur in role-play transcripts, would test hypothesis C.
          • The raw misconduct exception rests on 12 contexts, and labeling is partly to blame. It deserves a closer look with more prompts before anything is made of it.

          • Labels: GPT-5.4-mini (medium reasoning), frozen perspective-map rubric, batches of 8 with anonymised ids. Only clear labels are counted.
          • Behavior labels: GPT-5.4-mini, one label per unit, from the last 600 characters of the prompt and the 24-token continuation (rubric v2 in label_behavior.py). The first version passed the prompt with the continuation already appended, so the judge saw Qwen’s words twice and often read a fresh answer as “continuing the text”; it also credited the template’s empty block to Qwen. Re-judging with the prompt alone moved 201 of 2,028 labels from “continues the text” to “answers as assistant”. A continuation without a <think> tag can no longer be labeled as starting reasoning or writing an empty block. In a check of 35 labels (25 of them changed), 33 looked right; the two errors were continuations where Qwen writes a user’s next request.
          • Vectors: shared fraction, probes on 64 principal components (5-fold), cosine between arms on matched contexts.
          Label quality and data issues
          • Blind check: 80 readouts (40 blank line, 40 control) read blind; 77/80 agreement with the labeler (κ = 0.92), 36/40 vs 0/40 themed by the blind reading.
          • Test–retest: a leftover labeling process ran in parallel and labeled about 26,000 readouts twice. The two labels agree on “has a theme” in 97% of cases (κ = 0.88). The analysis uses the first label; using the second moves small cells (24 reads) by up to 8 points and nothing else noticeably.
          • Overlapping controls: in Stage 0, the “\n\n outside the block” control originally included the \n\n right before and after the block. Those 704 duplicate jobs were assigned to the block-adjacent sites and removed from the control.
          • Exclusions: 49 readouts matched the safety filter and were never read or displayed. Examples on this page are seeded random draws and never come from the misconduct contexts.
